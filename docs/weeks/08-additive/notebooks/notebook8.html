Before the seminar

The lecture proved the variance of an average of correlated variables; exercise 1 is that proof again, with the slides closed. It derived boosting as approximate gradient descent; exercise 2 takes one boosting step by hand. Do both on paper before the seminar; the seminar’s problems are variations on them. You may use, without proof: \(\mathrm{Var}\big(\sum_b V_b\big) = \sum_b\sum_{b'}\mathrm{Cov}(V_b, V_{b'})\), and \(\mathrm{Cov}(V, V) = \mathrm{Var}(V)\).

Exercise 1 (correlated-average variance, with the slides closed). \(V_1, \ldots, V_B\) are random variables, each with variance \(\tau^2 > 0\), and every pair has correlation \(\rho\).

  1. Show that \(\mathrm{Var}\big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B V_b\big) = \rho\tau^2 + (1 - \rho)\tau^2/B\).

  2. What is left as \(B \to \infty\)? What does the formula give when \(\rho = 1\), and when \(\rho = 0\)?

  3. In a bagged ensemble or a random forest, \(V_b\) is the \(b\)th tree’s prediction at one input \(x\), and the randomness is the draw of the training data and of the resampling. Which term can more trees shrink, and which term does a random forest try to shrink instead?

NoteHint 1
  1. Write the variance of the sum as a double sum of covariances. How many terms have \(b = b'\), and how many have \(b \ne b'\)? What is each one?
NoteHint 2
  1. The covariance of two variables with correlation \(\rho\) and variances \(\tau^2\) is \(\rho\tau^2\). Divide the variance of the sum by \(B^2\), then write \(\frac{B(B-1)}{B^2} = 1 - \frac1B\) and regroup.

The training loss of fitted values \(\mathbf f = (f_1, \ldots, f_5)\) is \(L(\mathbf f) = \sum_i \frac12(y_i - f_i)^2\).

  1. Start from \(f_i = \bar y\) for every film. Compute \(\bar y\) and the negative gradient of \(L\) at this \(\mathbf f\).

  2. Fit a tree with one split on year to the negative gradient: for each of the four cutoffs halfway between consecutive years, compute the residual sum of squares of the two leaves, each predicting its mean. Which cutoff wins, and what does each leaf predict?

(b), (c) A leaf predicts the mean of the residuals in it. The step is \(f_i \leftarrow f_i + \gamma\,t(x_i)\), with \(t\) the tree.

Problem 1 (Andor). The lecture grew a tree on the five Star Wars films in the data (exercise 2’s table), splitting on year:

  • before 1991: 8.8 if the year is before 1981.5, 8.1 otherwise;

Problem 2 (the size of a boosting step). A boosting fit has fitted values \(\mathbf f = (f_1, \ldots, f_n)\) and residuals \(r_i = y_i - f_i\), with training loss \(L(\mathbf f) = \sum_i \frac12(y_i - f_i)^2\). A tree \(t\) with leaves \(l = 1, \ldots, m\) is fitted to the residuals: in leaf \(l\), which holds \(n_l\) of the units, it predicts the mean \(\bar r_l\) of their residuals. Write \(t_i\) for its prediction for unit \(i\).

  1. Show that \(L(\mathbf f + \gamma\,\mathbf t) = L(\mathbf f) - \gamma\big(1 - \frac\gamma2\big)\sum_{l=1}^m n_l\,\bar r_l^{\,2}\).

  2. For which \(\gamma \ge 0\) does a step not raise the training loss? Which \(\gamma\) lowers it most?

  3. The five residuals \(0.98, 0.98, 0.28, -1.42, -0.82\), with a tree whose leaves hold the first three and the last two. Compute \(\sum_l n_l\bar r_l^{\,2}\) and the loss after a step of \(\gamma = 0.5\), \(1\) and \(2\).

  4. Part (b) gives the \(\gamma\) that lowers the training loss most at every step. Why do boosting libraries use a much smaller \(\gamma\) by default?

Problem 3 (what a larger search costs). A model’s risk is its expected loss on a new unit, \(R = \mathbb E[\ell]\), for a loss with \(0 \le \ell \le 1\) (for example, the 0–1 loss of a classifier). Before the tuning data are seen, \(K\) candidate models are fixed, fitted on the training data, with risks \(R_1, \ldots, R_K\); treat the fitted models as fixed, so that everything below is conditional on the training data. On \(m\) tuning units, drawn independently of each other and of the training data, candidate \(k\) scores \(\hat R_k\), the mean of its \(m\) losses. You may use Hoeffding’s inequality (Hoeffding 1963): if \(Z_1, \ldots, Z_m\) are independent with \(0 \le Z_i \le 1\) and \(\bar Z\) is their mean, then for every \(t > 0\), \[ P\big(|\bar Z - \mathbb E\bar Z| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-2mt^2}. \]

  1. \(K = 1\). Find the \(t\) for which \(|\hat R_1 - R_1| < t\) with probability at least \(1 - \delta\).

  2. Find \(t_K\) such that, with probability at least \(1 - \delta\), \(|\hat R_k - R_k| < t_K\) for every \(k\) at once. Did you need the \(K\) scores to be independent of each other?

  3. Let \(\hat k\) be the candidate with the lowest tuning score and \(k^\star\) the candidate with the lowest risk. On the event of (b), show that \(R_{\hat k} < \hat R_{\hat k} + t_K\) and \(R_{\hat k} < R_{k^\star} + 2t_K\).

  4. Take \(m = 2000\) and \(\delta = 0.05\). Compute \(t_K\) for \(K = 1\), \(10\), \(100\) and \(1{,}000{,}000\). How many candidates double \(t_1\)? How many tuning units halve \(t_K\)? What does this say about searching a larger grid?

  5. The argument needs the \(K\) candidates to be fixed before any tuning score is seen. Give an example of a search that breaks this.

1. A random forest, two knobs

rf_fit(mtry, nodesize) fits a forest of 300 trees on the training films. Its two knobs: mtry, the number of inputs tried at each split (randomForest’s default for 9 inputs is 3), and nodesize, the size below which a node is not split (default 5). rf_score() returns a forest’s MSE on the tuning films.

rf_fit <- function(mtry, nodesize) {
  randomForest(rating ~ year + length + Action + Animation + Comedy + Drama + Documentary + Romance + Short,
               data = mv_train, ntree = 300, mtry = mtry, nodesize = nodesize)
}
rf_score <- function(mtry, nodesize) mse(mv_tune$rating, predict(rf_fit(mtry, nodesize), mv_tune))

2. Boosted trees, three knobs

xgboost has three knobs that matter most here: the depth of each tree, the learning rate \(\gamma\), and the number of iterations. Its defaults are depth 6, rate 0.3 and 100 iterations. One fit of 800 iterations gives the predictions after any smaller number, so the chunk below fits 12 models (4 depths, 3 rates) and scores each after 5, 10, 25, …, 800 iterations: 96 models in a table, models, with each one’s MSE on the tuning films. loss holds every model’s squared errors on the 2,000 tuning films, one column per model, in the order of the rows of models; col is that column. (About 5 seconds.)

X_train <- as.matrix(select(mv_train, -title, -rating))
X_tune <- as.matrix(select(mv_tune, -title, -rating))
X_test <- as.matrix(select(mv_test, -title, -rating))
boost_fit <- function(depth, rate) {
  xgboost(x = X_train, y = mv_train$rating, nrounds = 800, max_depth = depth, learning_rate = rate, nthread = 1)
}
iters <- c(5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800)
models <- expand_grid(depth = c(1, 2, 4, 6), rate = c(0.03, 0.1, 0.3), iters = iters) |>
  mutate(col = row_number())
loss <- matrix(NA, nrow(mv_tune), nrow(models))
for (d in c(1, 2, 4, 6)) for (r in c(0.03, 0.1, 0.3)) {
  fit <- boost_fit(d, r)
  for (b in iters) {
    j <- models |> filter(depth == d, rate == r, iters == b) |> pull(col)
    loss[, j] <- (mv_tune$rating - predict(fit, X_tune, iteration_range = c(1, b)))^2
  }
}
models$tune_mse <- colMeans(loss)
